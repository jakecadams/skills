# To-CEO brief: raw source material

Research base for the to-ceo skill. Gathered 2026-09-01.

Primary texts read in full: the Completed Staff Work memo, Churchill's
Brevity memo, the Axios Smart Brevity 101 guide, and the official
Writing for Busy Readers six-principles checklist. The books (Minto,
Grove, Mochary, Bryar & Carr, Rogers & Lasky-Fink) are represented
through detailed published notes and excerpts, with quotes verbatim
where sources gave them. Each section ends with the rules distilled
from that source. The Convergence section at the end is synthesis, not
source material.

## 1. The Doctrine of Completed Staff Work (US Army, 1942)

Drafted for the Provost Marshal General, U.S. Army, by Colonel Archer
J. Lerch, Deputy Provost General. Published in the January 29, 1942
issue of the Army and Navy Journal. Reproduced countless times by
military and civilian organizations since. Full text:

> 1. The doctrine of "completed staff work" is a doctrine of this
> office.
>
> 2. "Completed Staff Work" is the study of a problem, and
> presentation of a solution, by a staff officer, in such form that
> all that remains to be done on the part of the head of the staff
> division, or the commander, is to indicate his approval or
> disapproval of the completed action. The words "completed action"
> are emphasized because the more difficult the problem is, the more
> the tendency is to present the problem to the chief in piece-meal
> fashion. It is your duty as a staff officer to work out the
> details. You should not consult your chief in the determination of
> those details, no matter how perplexing they may be. You may and
> should consult other staff officers. The product, whether it
> involves the pronouncement of a new policy or effects an
> established one, should, when presented to the chief for approval
> or disapproval, be worked out in finished form.
>
> 3. The impulse which often comes to the inexperienced staff officer
> to ask the chief what to do, recurs more often when the problem is
> difficult. It is accompanied by a feeling of mental frustration. It
> is so easy to ask the chief what to do, and it appears so easy for
> him to answer. Resist that impulse. You will succumb to it only if
> you do not know your job. It is your job to advise your chief what
> he ought to do, not to ask him what you ought to do. He needs
> answers, not questions. Your job is to study, write, restudy and
> rewrite until you have evolved a single proposed action — the best
> one of all you have considered. Your chief merely approves or
> disapproves.
>
> 4. Do not worry your chief with long explanations and memoranda.
> Writing a memorandum to your chief does not constitute completed
> staff work, but writing a memorandum for your chief to send to
> someone else does. Your views should be placed before him in
> finished form so that he can make them his views by simply signing
> his name. In most instances, completed staff work results in a
> single document prepared for the signature of the chief, without
> accompanying comment. If the proper result is reached, the chief
> will usually recognize it at once. If he wants comment or
> explanation, he will ask for it.
>
> 5. The theory of completed staff work does not preclude a "rough
> draft" but the rough draft must not be a half-baked idea. It must
> be complete in every respect except that it lacks the requisite
> number of copies and need not be neat. But a rough draft must not
> be used as an excuse for shifting to the chief the burden of
> formulating the action.
>
> 6. The "completed staff work" theory may result in more work for
> the staff officer, but it results in more freedom for the chief.
> This is as it should be. Further, it accomplishes two things:
>     a. The chief is protected from half-baked ideas, voluminous
>     memoranda, and immature oral presentments.
>     b. The staff officer who has a real idea to sell is enabled
>     more readily to find a market.
>
> 7. When you have finished your "completed staff work" the final
> test is this:
>     If you were the chief would you be willing to sign the paper
>     you have prepared, and stake your professional reputation on
>     its being right?
>     If the answer is in the negative, take it back and work it
>     over, because it is not yet "completed staff work."

Rules distilled:

- Present a solution, not a problem. The reader's only remaining act
  is approve or disapprove.
- Hard problems tempt you to deliver piecemeal. Resist; the harder
  the problem, the more finished the product must be.
- Work out every detail yourself. Consult peers, not the chief.
- Study, write, restudy, rewrite until one proposed action remains —
  the best of all you considered.
- No long explanations attached. If the reader wants explanation,
  they will ask.
- A rough draft is allowed; a half-baked idea is not. Rough means
  unpolished, never incomplete.
- The trade is explicit: more work for the writer, more freedom for
  the reader.
- Final test: would you sign it yourself and stake your reputation
  on it being right?

## 2. Winston Churchill, "Brevity" (9 August 1940)

Memo to the War Cabinet, three months into his premiership, during
the Battle of Britain. Full text:

> **Brevity**
>
> To do our work, we all have to read a mass of papers. Nearly all
> of them are far too long. This wastes time, while energy has to be
> spent in looking for the essential points.
>
> I ask my colleagues and their staffs to see to it that their
> Reports are shorter.
>
> 1. The aim should be Reports which set out the main points in a
> series of short, crisp paragraphs.
>
> 2. If a Report relies on detailed analysis of some complicated
> factors, or on statistics, these should be set out in an Appendix.
>
> 3. Often the occasion is best met by submitting not a full-dress
> Report, but an Aide-memoire consisting of headings only, which can
> be expanded orally if needed.
>
> 4. Let us have an end of such phrases as these: "It is also of
> importance to bear in mind the following considerations…", or
> "Consideration should be given to the possibility of carrying into
> effect…". Most of these woolly phrases are mere padding, which can
> be left out altogether, or replaced by a single word. Let us not
> shrink from using the short expressive phrase, even if it is
> conversational.
>
> Reports drawn up on the lines I propose may at first seem rough as
> compared with the flat surface of officialese jargon. But the
> saving in time will be great, while the discipline of setting out
> the real points concisely will prove an aid to clearer thinking.
>
> W.S.C.

Rules distilled:

- Main points in short, crisp paragraphs.
- Complicated analysis and statistics go to an appendix.
- The minimum viable report is headings only, expanded orally on
  request (the aide-memoire).
- Padding phrases get deleted or replaced by a single word. The
  short conversational phrase is allowed.
- The closing claim: concision is a discipline that produces clearer
  thinking, not only shorter reading.

## 3. BLUF — Bottom Line Up Front (Army Regulation 25-50)

The standard for US Army correspondence. AR 25-50 mandates that
"Army writing will be concise, organized, and to the point," with
two required features: the main point at the beginning, and active
voice. In 2017 Defense Secretary Jim Mattis directed the DoD to
"Give members of Congress the Bottom Line Up Front; be direct and to
the point using clear, concise, and straightforward language."

Construction of a BLUF message:

1. The BLUF statement — the key message or required action, first.
2. Supporting context — how the conclusion was reached.
3. Evidence — facts, events, or prior decisions justifying it.

The BLUF answers the five Ws quickly: who, what, where, when, why.

Example from practice:

> "BLUF: I need you to approve both the design and content of the
> attached flyer by noon on August 10."

Military email convention pairs BLUF with keyword subject lines:
ACTION, INFO, DECISION, REQUEST, SIGN — the reader knows the
required response before opening.

Related forms: journalism's inverted pyramid (importance-ordered
information, conclusion in the lede) and TL;DR. BLUF is simpler and
shorter than a formal executive summary.

Rules distilled:

- The conclusion or required action is the first sentence.
- Label the response you need (action, decision, information).
- Active voice, direct statements.
- Everything after the bottom line is support, ordered by
  importance.

## 4. Barbara Minto — The Pyramid Principle

Developed at McKinsey in the 1960s; the house method of every major
consulting firm since.

**The pyramid.** Three tiers:

1. Top: the governing thought — one clear answer to the reader's
   question ("You should X").
2. Middle: three to four key arguments supporting it, grouped by
   theme.
3. Base: evidence — data, findings, analysis — under each argument.

**Think up, communicate down.** "The thinking process is done from
the ground up," while "communicating is done in the reverse, sharing
it from the top down." Research bottom-up; do not start writing
until the top of the pyramid is clear; then present answer-first.
Focus on findings, not your investigative journey.

**Four logic rules:**

- Answer first: "Do not build up to the conclusion. Deliver it
  immediately to provide context for the details that follow."
- Vertical logic: every point raises a question (why? how?) that
  the points directly below must answer.
- Horizontal logic: grouped points belong to one logical category,
  ordered chronologically, structurally, or by importance.
- MECE: groupings are mutually exclusive (no overlaps) and
  collectively exhaustive (no gaps).

**SCQA introduction.** The entry point that frames the pyramid:

- Situation: uncontroversial baseline facts the reader already
  accepts.
- Complication: what changed, broke, or is at stake.
- Question: the question the complication raises.
- Answer: the governing thought, delivered immediately.

**Headlines substantiate, not label.** Not "Three reasons we should
do X" but "We must do X because it aligns with our strategy,
supports industry trends and plays to our strengths." Summary
headlines carry the claim.

**Deductive vs inductive.** Deductive chains (premise → premise →
conclusion) suit resistant audiences; inductive groupings (parallel
observations under one theme) absorb faster and survive challenges
to a single support. Consulting default: inductive.

**Known limitations.** All-or-nothing risk — reject the top claim
and the whole case falls. And the form is informational, not
collaborative; it presents a finished position rather than
co-designing one.

Rules distilled:

- One governing thought per document, stated first.
- Three to four supports, MECE, each a claim, not a category label.
- Every level answers the question the level above raises.
- Set context only through SCQA, and keep it short.
- Write nothing until the top of the pyramid is decided.

## 5. Andy Grove — High Output Management (1983)

**Reports.** The famous inversion:

> "Reports are more a medium of self-discipline than a way to
> communicate information. Writing the report is important; reading
> it often is not."

The value is in the preparation forcing the writer to think, an
argument Bezos later repeats for narratives.

**Indicators.** A manager runs on measurements:

- Measure output, not activity: "you measure a salesman by the
  orders he gets (output), not by the calls he makes (activity)."
- Indicators should be physical, countable things.
- Pair indicators so their counter-effects show: quantity paired
  with quality, so attention cannot skew one way.
- Include leading indicators that show direction, not only results.
- Grove's warning: people "act on leading indicators of good news,
  but only act on lagging indicators of bad news." Negative leading
  indicators are systematically under-weighted, so surface them
  first.

**One-on-ones.** The subordinate sets the agenda and does most of
the talking. The agenda generally covers:

- Performance figures and indicators, with emphasis on indicators
  that signal trouble.
- Anything important since the last meeting: hiring, people
  problems, organizational problems, future plans.
- Potential problems — things that nag even before they are
  tangible.

Supervisor technique, the Principle of Didactic Management: "Ask one
more question!" When the subordinate seems done, another question
keeps the real issue surfacing.

**Meetings.** "A meeting is nothing less than the medium through
which managerial work is performed." Staff meetings cover only
matters affecting two or more people present; one-on-one issues get
redirected.

**Decision-making.** Three steps: free discussion (all views as
equals), clear decision (especially when disagreement exists), full
support from everyone. Decide at the lowest competent level. Before
deciding, state what decision is needed, by when, who decides, who
consults, who ratifies, who is informed.

**Leverage.** Managerial output = organizational output + influence
on neighboring organizations. High-leverage acts affect many people
or shift one person's behavior long-term. A lack of decision is a
negative decision.

**MBO.** Two questions: where do I want to go (objective); how will
I pace myself getting there (key results). Few objectives; specific
key results.

Rules distilled:

- The report disciplines the writer; do not confuse writing it with
  communicating.
- Report indicators, output over activity, paired to prevent
  gaming, with trouble signals weighted first.
- Bad news gets acted on late by default; put negative leading
  indicators at the top.
- The person reporting sets the agenda and flags potential problems
  before they are tangible.
- Decisions need a named decider, a date, and full support once
  made.

## 6. Matt Mochary — The Great CEO Within (2019)

**When you say it twice, write it down.** Any repeated explanation
signals documentation need. "To vastly improve the quality of the
communication and reduce the amount of time that you spend
communicating the information, write it down." Team-wide knowledge
goes to a wiki.

**Writing versus talking.** For important decisions, anyone
proposing an idea does so in writing before the meeting, following
Bezos: anyone who wants to bring up an issue or proposal must write
it up fully before the decision meeting. Phased adoption:

1. Phase 1: first 15 minutes of the meeting to write updates and
   issues; 10 minutes to read one another's; then discuss.
2. Phase 2: written updates required before the meeting; nothing
   unwritten may be raised.
3. Phase 3: updates due at a set time the prior day (for example
   6 p.m.); all participants read and comment in writing before the
   meeting; meeting time is for finalizing decisions only.

**Issue write-up format.** Never allow a problem to be raised
without a suggested solution. Each issue is written as:

1. Fact: objective, observable actions only — what a video camera
   would capture.
2. Story: the thoughts, opinions, and judgments about those facts,
   labeled as story.
3. Proposed solution: specific actions with a directly responsible
   individual (DRI) and due date, stated boldly as "I propose we do
   X."

Even at low confidence, propose: take a guess with 10 percent
confidence rather than bring an open question.

**Decision methods, matched to stakes:**

| Method | Speed | Buy-in | Use for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Manager decides alone | Fast | Minimal | Trivial decisions |
| Straw man proposal, then feedback | Medium | Medium | Most decisions |
| Group consensus | Slow | Maximum | Major visionary decisions |

Reversible decisions are made below the CEO; irreversible ones by
the CEO. RAPID (Bain) assigns Recommend, Agree, Perform, Input,
Decide. The decision is documented with next actions, DRIs, due
dates, and published company-wide.

**Loudest voice in the room.** Capture views in writing before
discussion; junior speaks first, leadership last; the senior person
says "Before I give my opinion, I want to hear from each of you."

**Impeccable agreements.** Precisely defined (what, who, when
explicit) and fully agreed in writing. Not "John will handle
marketing" but "John will deliver the Q4 marketing plan draft by
Oct 10, emailed to the team." Broken agreements get flagged to all
parties immediately and renegotiated, never silently dropped.

**Transparency.** Share everything positive and negative except
compensation and performance reviews. "Our imaginations are more
powerful (and often more dire) than reality." If someone must
attend a meeting to know what happened in it, the system is not yet
transparent — publish the notes.

**Feedback script (5 steps).** Ask permission; state the behavior
as fact; state the impact and feeling; state your story labeled as
story; make a positive request ("do X" not "don't do Y") and ask
for acceptance. Praise in public, criticize in private, never
negative feedback over text.

Rules distilled:

- Issues arrive written, with facts separated from story, and a
  proposed solution attached — guess even at 10% confidence.
- The write-up states "I propose we do X" and names a DRI and date.
- Written-first meetings: read and comment before, decide during.
- Commitments are impeccable: what, who, when, in writing.
- Share bad news fully; hidden gaps get filled by darker rumor.

## 7. Amazon — Working Backwards (Bryar & Carr, 2021)

Bezos banned PowerPoint for S-team meetings in June 2004, inspired
by Edward Tufte's "The Cognitive Style of PowerPoint." Tufte's
charge: PowerPoint was designed for one-to-many ballroom
presentations, not for small groups of senior executives deciding
issues that are "causal, complex, multivariate, comparative,
evidence-based and resolution intense." Slides gloss over critical
details and reward presentation skill over analysis. Bezos:
"There is no way to write a six-page narratively structured memo
and not have clear thinking."

**Six-pager rules:**

- Six pages maximum for a one-hour meeting (15-20 minute read);
  three pages for a 30-minute meeting.
- Complete sentences and paragraphs — narrative prose, no bullet
  fragments. Not "Sales +45% last month" but "Sales increased to
  $37.3 MM (+45% versus prior month and +15% YoY)."
- Precise data over subjective language: never "strong growth"
  where a number exists.
- Every sentence must add value; the goal is six pages with no
  filler, not padding to six.
- Appendix is optional and never required reading; only the six
  pages are read in the meeting.
- Typical sections (adapted per topic): introduction, goals,
  tenets, state of the business, lessons learned, strategic
  priorities.

**The silent meeting.** About 25% of the meeting is silent
individual reading with inline comments; 75% is discussion. The
author addresses comments sequentially or page-by-page. Senior
leaders speak last to avoid anchoring the room.

**PR/FAQ.** The working-backwards variant for new products: a
one-page future press release announcing the finished product in
customer language, followed by frequently asked questions —
customer FAQs, then internal/stakeholder FAQs with the hard
numbers. Writing the announcement first forces the customer benefit
to exist before the work does.

Rules distilled:

- For decisions, depth wins: full narrative, complete sentences,
  exact numbers, hard page cap.
- The cap is load-bearing in both directions: no filler to reach
  it, no overflow past it; overflow detail goes to an appendix that
  is never required reading.
- Numbers replace adjectives.
- The document is the presentation; the meeting is the discussion.

## 8. Axios — Smart Brevity (VandeHei, Allen, Schwartz, 2022)

From the official Smart Brevity 101 guide (Axios HQ). The premise:
"We're living in a world with way too many words." We check our
phones over 200 times a day, decide within 17 milliseconds whether
we like what we clicked, and spend under 30 seconds on average
reading whatever we do read. "Most Smart Brevity communications are
about 50% shorter than traditional styles — with all the same
important substance." (The checklist page says 40% shorter; the 101
guide says 50%.)

**The six steps, verbatim headings:**

1. **Audience first — always.** Before writing, answer: what is
   the goal, who is the ideal reader, who is a real person fitting
   that profile? Keep only what is new, interesting, or urgent to
   that person. Cut anything familiar.
2. **Grab their attention.** Headline or subject line: under 60
   characters, concrete (the reader learns something from it),
   conversational.
3. **Say "What's new" and "Why it matters."** Pick the single most
   important detail, sum it up in one sentence, say it first —
   always. Busy readers ask two questions of new information: What
   is this? Is it relevant to me? Answer both fast.
4. **Write like a human.** The words you would say over coffee are
   the ones to write. Short, simple language reads as confidence
   and is more memorable.
5. **Stay scannable.** 60-80% of people scan rather than read long
   text (University of Maryland research). Short paragraphs,
   bolding, bullets; subject-verb-object sentences.
6. **Stop when enough is enough.** As few words, sentences, and
   paragraphs as possible. "The greatest gift that you can give to
   yourself — and others — is time."

Closing line: "Smart Brevity is the art of being short, not
shallow."

**The unit structure** (from the book and Axios house style): a
tease/headline of six or so words; one strong first sentence (the
lede) with the one big thing; a bolded "Why it matters:" line of
context; then "Go deeper:" — the opt-in link or section for detail.
Axioms in bold ("The big picture:", "The bottom line:", "What's
next:") label each paragraph's function so the reader can skip by
label.

Rules distilled:

- One big thing per update, in one sentence, first.
- "Why it matters" is written out, not implied.
- Depth exists but is opt-in ("Go deeper"), never mandatory.
- Bold functional labels make the structure scannable.
- Cut everything the reader already knows.

## 9. Rogers & Lasky-Fink — Writing for Busy Readers (2023)

The evidence base. Rogers is a Harvard behavioral scientist;
Lasky-Fink is research director at Harvard's People Lab.

**How busy readers actually read.** They skim, and they quit early.
Length itself signals difficulty: readers interpret a long message
"as an indication of how difficult and time-consuming it will be to
respond to," and many never come back.

**The experiments:**

- 7,002 school board members: a 127-word email got a 2.7% survey
  response rate; the 49-word version got 4.8% — nearly double.
- Perception: 29% of readers of the concise version guessed the
  survey would take under 5 minutes; 15% for the wordy version.
- Editing study (Adams et al., University of Virginia): 83% of
  participants added words when asked to edit; people expand by
  default.
- 165 of 166 professionals chose to deal with the concise message
  first.

Standard: each word must earn its place in the sentence, each
sentence in the paragraph, each idea in the text.

**The six principles with all sub-rules (official checklist,
verbatim):**

1. **Less Is More** — use fewer words; include fewer ideas; make
   fewer requests.
2. **Make Reading Easy** — use short and common words; write
   straightforward sentences; write shorter sentences.
3. **Design for Easy Navigation** — make key information
   immediately visible; separate distinct ideas; place related
   ideas together; order ideas by priority; include headings;
   consider using visuals.
4. **Use Enough Formatting but No More** — match formatting to
   readers' expectations; highlight, bold, or underline the most
   important ideas; limit your formatting.
5. **Tell Readers Why They Should Care** — emphasize what readers
   value ("So what?"); emphasize which readers should care ("Why
   me?").
6. **Make Responding Easy** — simplify the steps required to act;
   organize key information needed for action; minimize the amount
   of attention required.

Rules distilled:

- Fewer words, fewer ideas, fewer requests — all three, not only
  words.
- Length up front is a cost the reader prices before reading; long
  messages get deferred or skipped entirely.
- Formatting is an attention budget: highlight the critical,
  strip it from everything else, or the highlights stop working.
- The ask must be effortless to act on: steps simplified,
  information gathered, attention minimized.

## 10. Jacob Kaplan-Moss — "How to Give a Status Update to
Executives" (2021)

Origin story: as a young engineer he prepared 24 slides for an
executive briefing on a security incident and was then told his
slot ran 1:35-1:38 pm — three minutes. The template that survived:

1. **Overall summary** — start with the feeling ("running
   smoothly" / "I'm worried about…"), then substantiate with the
   number ("we're two weeks ahead of schedule").
2. **Highlights** — one or two milestones, ships, or standout
   people.
3. **Risks** — one or two concerns the executives need to know:
   budget, schedule, production.
4. **Request** — the specific guidance or decision needed from
   them.
5. **Close** — "any questions?"

His sample opening:

> "Project X is running smoothly. We're making steady progress:
> we've delivered a bit over half of the features on our roadmap,
> and we're on track to launch publicly in May."

Philosophy: a good update is "very short — a few minutes, max," a
handful of key points. Information is need-to-know; comprehensive
detail is not proof of competence — filtering is. Executives "value
people who filter for 'what you need to know.'" But master the
details anyway: follow-up questions must land on solid ground.

Rules distilled:

- Verdict (with its number) first, in one or two sentences.
- One or two of each: highlights, risks. Not lists of everything.
- The request is its own section, explicit.
- Brevity in the report, depth in reserve for questions.

## 11. Managing up — the no-surprises rule (Rework guide)

**No surprises.** The boss hears about problems from you first,
early enough to be useful. Cited McKinsey research ranks
no-surprises as the single most consistent predictor of upward
trust — above technical competence and above business results.
The heads-up script: "Heads up. There was friction over [issue].
I've addressed it, but wanted you to know before [meeting]."

**Reporting a problem.** Three parts together: "Here's what's
happening, here's what I think is going on, here's what I'm
planning to do about it." Situation, your read, your plan — never
the situation alone.

**Status-risk-ask weekly format:**

- Status: two or three factual outcomes from the week.
- Risk: one or two developing items to watch.
- Ask: one explicit request for input or decision, or "no ask this
  week."

Extended template: This week (decisions, milestones) / On my radar
(developing situations) / FYI (external developments) / What I
might need (only when real). Three to five short paragraphs; a
90-second skim.

**Format follows the reader.** Ask how they want updates — written
and regular, or real-time when something happens — and honor it
even if it differs from your preference.

Rules distilled:

- Bad news travels to the reader before any other channel carries
  it.
- A problem report is always situation + your read + your plan.
- Every update ends in an explicit ask or an explicit "no ask."
- The reader's preferred format wins.

## Convergence (synthesis, not source material)

Every source teaches the same five moves:

| Move | Sources |
|---|---|
| Verdict first — answer, status, or recommendation in the first sentence, with its number | Minto, BLUF, Smart Brevity, inverted pyramid, Kaplan-Moss |
| Recommend, don't ask — bring one proposed action, your read, your confidence | Staff work ("He needs answers, not questions"), Mochary (propose at 10% confidence), Rework (situation + read + plan) |
| Bad news earliest — negative leading indicators at the top, never below the fold | Grove, no-surprises rule, Kaplan-Moss risks section |
| Depth is opt-in — short crisp main points; analysis in an appendix, link, or on request | Churchill, Smart Brevity "Go deeper", Amazon appendix rule, Rogers (length is a price the reader sees first) |
| End with the ask — one explicit request, or an explicit "no action needed" | BLUF keywords, Kaplan-Moss request section, Rework status-risk-ask, Rogers "make responding easy" |

Two genres, one standard. The report-back (Kaplan-Moss, Churchill,
Smart Brevity, status-risk-ask) is read in seconds; the decision
document (Amazon six-pager, Minto pyramid, completed staff work
product) is read in minutes and argued in full sentences. The
standard behind both is completed staff work: the thinking is
finished before the reader arrives. The report compresses finished
thinking; it never substitutes for it. Churchill's aide-memoire
marks the floor of the genre: headings only, each carrying its
point, detail on request.

Writer discipline. Grove ("a medium of self-discipline"), Churchill
("an aid to clearer thinking"), and Bezos ("no way to write a
six-page memo and not have clear thinking") make the same claim
from three directions: the format constraint exists for the
writer's thinking first and the reader's time second.

Agent mapping. An agent reporting back is a staff officer. The
doctrine's banned behaviors are the standard agent failure modes:
piecemeal presentation of a hard problem (process narration),
voluminous memoranda (log dumps, everything-I-did lists), asking
the chief what to do (open questions without a recommendation),
half-baked ideas (unverified claims stated as findings), and
burying the negative leading indicator (blocker in paragraph four).
The reader-side evidence (Rogers) adds the price mechanism: a long
report gets skimmed or deferred, so an agent that reports at length
loses the reader before the blocker is reached.
