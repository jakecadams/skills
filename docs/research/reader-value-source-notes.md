# reader-value: raw source material

<!-- writing-style:disable — this file quotes sources verbatim -->

Research base for the reader-value skill. Gathered 2026-09-02/03.

Every quote below was read in a fetched page during research, not recalled.
Where a quote reached us second-hand, the entry says so. Quotes that failed
verification are recorded in "Corrections" at the end rather than deleted, so
the same errors do not get re-introduced later.

Origin: this file digests `effective-writing-skill-spec.md`, which proposed a
skill grounded in McEnerney, Wallace, Caro, Graham, Adams, and Bruce Lee. The
spec's source claims were checked against primary material; three needed
correction. Part 3 records what a repo audit found about overlap with the
skills already shipped here.

## 1. Larry McEnerney — The Craft of Writing Effectively

University of Chicago Writing Program. Read in full: the verbatim transcript of
the 9 May 2024 delivery (~12,700 words) at singjupost.com. Also read: his
UChicago Core interview, and a note-taker's quote-heavy record of the companion
lecture "Writing Beyond the Academy" (2015), marked below where used.

McEnerney is the spine of this skill. His argument is not "consider your
reader" — it is a mechanism, and the spec omitted the mechanism entirely.

### The argument

Expert writers were trained in a system where readers were paid to care about
them, so they learned to produce texts that display the inside of their heads.
Outside school nobody is paid to read you. Readers are not looking for your
thinking; they are looking for something valuable to them, and "valuable" is
not a property of your text — it is a judgment made by one specific community.

> "Teachers read text because they are paid to care about the students. You've
> learned to write in a system where you're writing to readers who are paid to
> care about you. That will stop."

> "You think writing is conveying your ideas. It's not. Let me say that again.
> You think that writing is communicating your ideas to your readers. It is
> not."

> "It's not conveying your ideas to your readers. It's changing their ideas.
> Nobody cares what ideas you have."

> "There's no such thing as value here. Value is here. The question is whether
> this particular community of readers values it, which is why it's so much
> about readers and not about content."

Clarity ranks below value, but he does not dismiss it:

> "And the other stuff doesn't matter. If it's clear and useless, it's useless.
> If it's organized and useless, it's useless."

His full ordering in the lecture: writing needs to be clear, organized, and
persuasive — "This is way more important than this, but more than anything
else from now on, your writing needs to be valuable. Because if it's not that,
nothing else matters." Value is a precondition, not a substitute.

### The mechanism: instability, then cost

This is the operational core, and the part the spec left out.

> "So the language that people use is a language of continuity and consistency.
> The horrible irony is, your readers are actually searching for a language of
> instability, inconsistency, and tension."

Writers reach for continuity to show their work is sound, handing readers the
opposite of what readers hunt for. The failure cascade he names:

> "You are interfering with their reading process. They're slowing down.
> They're getting confused. They're getting aggravated. And they can stop two
> paragraphs into your text. Two paragraphs. They're done."

Instability alone is not enough. It must cost the reader something:

> "What we mean is, not always, but very often, you need to code, use code
> language to your readers to show them that the instability imposes a cost on
> them. Not on you."

Readers' natural reply to a bare inconsistency: my work is full of
inconsistencies, most of which do not matter — is yours costing us anything?

### Background is not history; background is the problem

> "When you say background to people, they usually say, OK, I guess we have
> more history of this subject or something. They don't mean that. They mean, I
> can't figure out why this matters. The background they need is a problem."

> "Then, you move to a solution. And of course, notice your thesis can be in a
> solution only if the readers perceive the problem."

> "Line one, classic thing. Begin with a definition. Teachers love this. Begin
> with a definition. Because it tells the teachers that you, what? You know the
> definition. Don't begin with definition, guys."

He calls the school opening the "martini glass model": generalization, thesis,
generalization — resting on the assumption that knowledge sits on stable
foundations.

### Argue, don't explain

> "You have learned that what explaining is, it's revealing to the world the
> inside of your head. No one cares about the inside of your head."

> "And then you better have an argument. Not an explanation. Do not explain.
> Argue. You're talking to people who wrote this stuff. You don't have to
> explain it to them."

Explanation is legitimate only after value is established and persuasion has
begun. He replaces the teacherly "Why do you think that?" with "Why should I
think that?"

### Error frame beats gap frame

> "If knowledge is infinite, and you filled in a gap, how many gaps are left?
> You have done nothing."

The gap move ("we've studied X but not Y") assumes a bounded, crossword-puzzle
model of knowledge. The reliable move is the error move: tell the readers they
are wrong about something, then show what believing it costs them.

### Knowing the readers

> "If you do not know your readers, the particular people in a community, if
> you do not know these people, you are very unlikely to create value and you
> are very unlikely to be persuasive because persuasion depends on what they
> doubt."

> "I know the code. Every community has its own codes. The communities you're
> entering have their own codes."

His homework: 15 minutes a week, print articles from your field, circle every
word creating value for readers, build a word list to inject during revision.

### Two more

> "By definition, anything you write has the function of helping your readers
> understand better something they want to understand well."

> "The function of your writing is to move this conversation forward. It cannot
> do that if it's in your desk drawer."

From the UChicago Core interview, on what his program actually teaches:

> "We spend a lot of time in the Schoolhouse telling people that we're not
> giving them an alternative set of rules, we're giving them tools for
> predicting where their readers are going to struggle."

And, in the same interview, asked which writing rule he would most like to
destroy: "Undoubtedly the whole passive verb rule. A lot of bad stuff comes out
of that rule." Worth noting against `clear-writing`'s unconditional
active-voice rule.

Second-hand (from the 2015 "Writing Beyond the Academy" note-taker's record,
not a verbatim transcript — treat as supporting colour, not citable text):

> "Concision isn't the number of words on the page, it's how long it takes
> readers to process what's on the page."

> "your readers do not trust you. They don't trust you. They think you're gonna
> waste their time."

### Rules for an agent

- Name the specific reader community and what they currently doubt. Persuasion
  depends on the doubt, not on the topic.
- Open by constructing the problem in order: community, instability, cost to
  them. Testable check: does the opening contain an instability word (but,
  however, although, inconsistent, anomaly) and a cost or benefit to the reader?
- Use the error frame, not the gap frame. Rewrite "nobody has looked at X" into
  "you believe X, and here is what that costs you."
- Ask of each section: is this arguing, or displaying knowledge? Cut the
  displays.
- Do not open with a definition, or with history the reader does not need to
  feel the problem.

## 2. Paul Graham

All essays read in full at paulgraham.com this run: "How to Write Usefully"
(useful.html), "Write Simply" (simply.html), "Putting Ideas Into Words"
(words.html), "Writing, Briefly" (writing44.html), "The Age of the Essay"
(essay.html), "Good Writing" (goodwriting.html), "Writing and Speaking"
(speak.html), "Writes and Write-Nots".

### The four-factor gate

> "Useful writing tells people something true and important that they didn't
> already know, and tells them as unequivocally as possible."

> "In effect the four components are like numbers you can multiply together to
> get a score for usefulness."

Important × novel × correct × strong. Caution: Graham states the formula both
ways and hedges the multiplication — his own summary writes it with plus signs.
Treat "a zero on any factor kills the piece" as a heuristic derived from his
simile, not as his stated rule.

> "Useful writing makes claims that are as strong as they can be made without
> becoming false."

> "If you've stated an idea as strongly as you could without making it false,
> all anyone has to do is to exaggerate slightly what you said, and now it is
> false."

> "Sometimes it means telling them something they knew unconsciously but had
> never put into words. In fact those may be the more valuable insights,
> because they tend to be more fundamental."

### Qualification is information, not tax

The single most useful correction to a pure-subtraction skill:

> "Don't underestimate qualification. It's an important skill in its own right,
> not just a sort of tax you have to pay in order to avoid saying things that
> are false."

Precision and correctness are opposing forces: the more precise a claim, the
harder it is to keep true. A qualifier naming a scope, a source, or a degree of
certainty carries information. Only empty qualifiers get deleted.

### Never ship what you do not believe

> "if you write a bad sentence, you don't publish it. You delete it and try
> again. Often you abandon whole branches of four or five paragraphs. Sometimes
> a whole essay."

> "The reason you'll seem confident is that you are confident: you've cheated,
> by only publishing the things you're sure of."

> "Brevity is the diction of command."

> "If you don't learn anything from writing an essay, don't publish it."

That last line licenses not-sending as an outcome.

### Process

> "My strategy is loose, then tight. I write the first draft of an essay fast,
> trying out all kinds of ideas. Then I spend days rewriting it very carefully."

> "I'll often spend 2 weeks on an essay and reread drafts 50 times."

> "You have to pretend to be a neutral reader who knows nothing of what's in
> your head, only what you wrote."

Note the persona: a *neutral* reader who knows only the page. Better engineered
than the spec's "hostile busy reader", which supplies an emotion but no repair
move.

> "Putting ideas into words is a severe test. The first words you choose are
> usually wrong; you have to rewrite sentences over and over to get them exactly
> right."

> "Half the ideas that end up in an essay will be ones you thought of while you
> were writing it. Indeed, that's why I write them."

> "no one who hasn't written about a topic has fully formed ideas about it. And
> someone who never writes has no fully formed ideas about anything nontrivial."

Leslie Lamport, quoted by Graham in "Writes and Write-Nots":

> "If you're thinking without writing, you only think you're thinking."

### Write Simply

> "The less energy they expend on your prose, the more they'll have left for
> your ideas."

> "writing simply keeps you honest. If you say nothing simply, it will be
> obvious to everyone, including you."

> "There are of course times when you want to use a complicated sentence or
> fancy word for effect. But you should never do it by accident."

> "The main reason I write simply is that it offends me not to."

### Writing, Briefly — his own compressed list

> "Write a bad version 1 as fast as you can; rewrite it over and over; cut out
> everything unnecessary; write in a conversational tone; develop a nose for bad
> writing, so you can see and fix it in yours; imitate writers you like"

> "expect 80% of the ideas in an essay to happen after you start writing it, and
> 50% of those you start with to be wrong; be confident enough to cut"

> "start writing when you think of the first sentence; if a deadline forces you
> to start before that, just say the most important sentence first"

> "read your essays out loud to see (a) where you stumble over awkward phrases
> and (b) which bits are boring (the paragraphs you dread reading)"

> "write for a reader who won't read the essay as carefully as you do, just as
> pop songs are designed to sound ok on crappy car radios"

> "use simple, germanic words; learn to distinguish surprises from digressions;
> learn to recognize the approach of an ending, and when one appears, grab it."

### On essays specifically

> "An essay doesn't begin with a statement, but with a question. In a real
> essay, you don't take a position and defend it."

> "Essays should aim for maximum surprise."

This is the genre seam: essays open on a question, reports open on the answer.

### Diagnosis

> "I just think Ugh, this doesn't sound right; what do I mean to say here?"

> "something that seems clumsily written will usually have gotten the ideas
> wrong too."

> "Having good ideas is most of writing well. If you know what you're talking
> about, you can say it in the plainest words and you'll be perceived as having
> a good style."

Treat awkwardness as evidence of a defective idea until proven otherwise.

### Rules for an agent

- Score before sending: important to this reader, novel to them, correct, and
  as strong as it can be without becoming false. Fix the weak factor rather
  than polishing the others.
- Test each claim for maximum honest strength: try the next stronger phrasing
  and ask whether it becomes false.
- Never let through a sentence you do not believe. Delete it and try again; if
  the section cannot be made true, cut the section.
- Stopping condition: reread start to finish, fix everywhere something catches,
  repeat until nothing catches. Not a word-count target, not a fixed pass count.
- Keep qualifiers that carry scope, source, or certainty. Delete only the empty
  ones.
- Name one thing the reader will not already know. If there is none, do not send
  it.

## 3. Robert Caro

Read this run: the *Working* "Turn Every Page" chapter excerpt, the Paris Review
"The Art of Biography No. 5" (No. 216, Spring 2016, interviewed by James
Santel), a Kirkus interview, an NPR Morning Edition transcript, and C-SPAN2
closed captions (machine-generated — flagged below).

### Turn every page

His Newsday editor Alan Hathway, after Caro dug out a story:

> "Just remember," he said. "Turn every page. Never assume anything. Turn every
> goddamned page."

> "I didn't know someone from Princeton could do digging like this," he said.
> "From now on, you do investigative work."

### Compression before drafting — the load-bearing method

> "I can't start writing a book until I've thought it through and can see it
> whole in my mind. So before I start writing, I boil the book down to three
> paragraphs, or two or one — that's when it comes into view. That process might
> take weeks. And then I turn those paragraphs into an outline of the whole
> book. That's what you see up here on my wall now — twenty-seven typewritten
> pages."

> "Getting that boiled-down paragraph or two is terribly hard, but I have to
> tell you that my experience is that if you get it, the whole next seven years
> is easier."

The compression then governs every later decision:

> "Is this fitting in with those three paragraphs? How is it fitting in? What
> you just wrote is good, but it's not fitting in. So you have to throw it away
> or find a way to make it fit in."

"It's good" is not a reason to keep it.

### The ending

Caro does know his last line before drafting, and says so directly. From a
Kirkus interview:

> "I have to have it all outlined. I can't just start writing. I have to know
> the beginning to the end."

From C-SPAN2 (machine-generated captions — the wording is approximate, the
substance is corroborated by the Kirkus quote above):

> "i can't write a book until [I] know the last line. so the last lines of every
> book are important to me ... if i know the ending, i can outline right up to
> it"

Two refinements the spec's phrasing misses. First, he *discovers* the last line
in the material rather than choosing it as a target — the Power Broker's "why
weren't they grateful?" arrived while he sat listening to a talk on public
ingratitude toward great men. Second, "structure backward from the ending"
misdescribes the direction: he outlines and drafts *forward to a known
destination*. The destination is fixed first; the construction runs forward.

### Drafting

> "That was why I resolved to write my first drafts in longhand, slowest of the
> various means of committing thoughts to paper, before I started doing later
> drafts on the typewriter; that is why I still do my first few drafts in
> longhand today."

His Princeton adviser R. P. Blackmur, the line that produced the longhand rule:

> "You're never going to achieve what you want to, Mr. Caro, if you don't stop
> thinking with your fingers."

Directly relevant to a model that emits fluent first-pass prose: fluency is the
symptom the rule targets.

> "I try to do at least three pages a day. Some days you don't, but without some
> kind of quota, I think you're fooling yourself."

> "I'm not sure I ever think the writing is going well. Every day I reread what
> I wrote the day before, and I've learned from hard experience that it's a real
> mistake to get too confident about what I've written. I do so much writing and
> rewriting. ... I rewrite the galleys completely. I even rewrite in page
> proofs, which they don't actually allow you to do, but they've been very good
> to me. I'd rewrite in the finished book if I could."

### Concreteness over assertion

> "I felt I just had to try to show — to make readers not only see but
> understand and feel — what "human cost" meant."

> "If I make this scene true enough so that the reader can see it, then the
> reader will see into Lyndon Johnson without my having to give any lectures or
> half-baked psychoanalysis."

> "I thought, You have the scenes, but it's your job to make the reader feel the
> desperation. How do you do that? ... And I actually had a note card attached
> to the lamp on my desk here. ... This one said, Is there desperation on this
> page?"

> "So you keep saying, What would I see? Sometimes these people get angry
> because I'm asking the same question over and over again."

### Facts

> "There is no one truth, but there are an awful lot of objective facts. The
> more facts you get, the more facts you collect, the closer you come to
> whatever truth there is. The base of biography has to be facts."

> "And finding facts — through reading documents or through interviewing and
> re-interviewing — can't be rushed; it takes time. Truth takes time."

On interviewing, the discipline of not filling silence:

> "Interviews: silence is the weapon, silence and people's need to fill it — as
> long as the person isn't you, the interviewer. ... When I'm waiting for the
> person I'm interviewing to break a silence by giving me a piece of information
> I want, I write "SU" (for Shut UP!) in my notebook. If anyone were ever to
> look through my notebooks, he would find a lot of "SUs" there."

### Rules for an agent

- Before drafting anything substantial, compress the whole piece to three
  paragraphs, then one. If you cannot, you do not understand it yet — go back to
  the material rather than start drafting.
- Keep the compression visible while drafting. At each section: is this fitting
  in? Good and not fitting means cut it.
- Know the destination before starting, then construct forward to it.
- Turn every page: read the primary source, not the summary. Open the one you
  were about to skip.
- Replace every load-bearing abstraction with the concrete instance that proves
  it. If you write "significant risk", show one case in enough detail that the
  reader concludes it unprompted.
- Do not lecture to a conclusion you could stage. If the numbers are rendered
  well, cut the sentence explaining what they mean.
- Uncertainty is a research state, not a prose style. Get more verified fact
  rather than better hedging language.

## 4. David Foster Wallace

"Tense Present: Democracy, English, and the Wars over Usage" (Harper's, April
2001), read in full. Reprinted expanded as "Authority and American Usage" in
*Consider the Lobster*.

The spec attributes "style is an ethical relationship with the reader" to
Wallace. That is a stretch as stated: the essay is mostly about usage politics.
What it actually gives is better and more specific.

### Discourse Community — the real contribution

> ""Correct" English usage is, as a practical matter, a function of whom you're
> talking to and how you want that person to respond — not just to your
> utterance but also to you. In other words, a large part of the agenda of any
> communication is rhetorical and depends on what some rhet-scholars call
> "Audience" or "Discourse Community.""

> "the dialect you use depends mostly on what sort of Group your listener is
> part of and whether you wish to present yourself as a fellow member of that
> Group."

> "at least one component of all this interpersonal semantic judging involves
> acceptance, meaning not some touchy-feely emotional affirmation but actual
> acceptance or rejection of somebody's bid to be regarded as a peer, a member
> of somebody else's collective or community or Group."

The SNOOTlet — the child whose flawless Standard Written English gets him
beaten up on the playground:

> "What neither he nor his teacher realizes is that the SNOOTlet is deficient in
> Language Arts. He has only one dialect."

That is the diagnosis of default model prose: uniformly correct register,
applied indiscriminately, signalling membership in no community the reader
belongs to. An agent that writes a commit message, an exec memo, a bug report,
and a README in the same hour needs the multi-dialect rule more than it needs
another correctness rule.

### Why academic prose is bad — the motive account

> "it is when a scholar's vanity/insecurity leads him to write primarily to
> communicate and reinforce his own status as an Intellectual that his English
> is deformed by pleonasm and pretentious diction (whose function is to signal
> the writer's erudition) and by opaque abstraction (whose function is to keep
> anybody from pinning the writer down to a definite assertion that can maybe be
> refuted or shown to be silly)."

> "the obscurity and pretension of Academic English can be attributed in part to
> a disruption in the delicate rhetorical balance between language as a vector
> of meaning and language as a vector of the writer's own resume."

> "it's tempting to think AE's real purpose is concealment and its real
> motivation fear."

Opaque abstraction has a function: it prevents refutation. That is a sharper
test than "avoid jargon" — ask whether an abstraction is there to be precise or
to be unfalsifiable.

### Cost to the reader, and the ethical appeal

> "many of these solecisms require at least a couple extra nanoseconds of
> cognitive effort, a kind of rapid sift-and-discard process, before the
> recipient gets it. Extra work."

> "it simply seems more "considerate" to follow the rules of correct SWE ... Not
> just more considerate but more respectful somehow — both of your listener and
> of what you're trying to get across."

> "What the Ethical Appeal amounts to is a complex and sophisticated "Trust me."
> It's the boldest, most ambitious, and also most distinctively American of
> rhetorical Appeals, because it requires the rhetor to convince us not just of
> his intellectual acuity or technical competence but of his basic decency and
> fairness and sensitivity to the audience's own hopes and fears."

> "A Democratic Spirit is one that combines rigor and humility, i.e., passionate
> conviction plus sedulous respect for the convictions of others."

> "Equally tough is a D.S.'s criterion of 100 percent intellectual integrity —
> you have to be willing to look honestly at yourself and your motives for
> believing what you believe, and to do it more or less continually."

On jargon that earns its place, from footnote 29:

> "Discourse Community is an example of that rare kind of academic jargon that's
> actually a valuable addition to SWE because it captures something at once very
> complex and very specific that no other English term quite can."

And footnote 30, the honest test for whether a term is jargon at all:

> "whether something gets called a "subdialect" or "jargon" seems to depend on
> how much it annoys people outside its Discourse Community."

### Rules for an agent

- Name the discourse community before drafting and write in its dialect. There
  is no generically correct register.
- A term of art survives when replacing it would lose precision. It fails when
  its function is to signal expertise or to avoid a refutable claim.
- Ask of every abstraction: is this here to be precise, or to be unpinnable?
- Credibility is earned on the page, not assumed.

## 5. Scott Adams

"The Day You Became a Better Writer" (Dilbert Blog, June 2007, ~264 words). The
original host is dead (Typepad shut down 30 September 2025); read this run in a
PDF compilation, cross-checked against an independent reprint.

The whole essay is short enough to treat as one long quote. The load-bearing
lines:

> "Business writing is about clarity and persuasion. The main technique is
> keeping things simple. Simple writing is persuasive."

> "A good argument in five sentences will sway more people than a brilliant
> argument in a hundred sentences. Don't fight it."

> "Simple means getting rid of extra words. Don't write, "He was very happy"
> when you can write "He was happy." You think the word "very" adds something.
> It doesn't. Prune your sentences."

> "Write short sentences. Avoid putting multiple thoughts in one sentence.
> Readers aren't as smart as you'd think."

> "Your first sentence needs to grab the reader. Go back and read my first
> sentence to this post. I rewrote it a dozen times. It makes you curious.
> That's the key."

His own opening, which is the demonstration:

> "I went from being a bad writer to a good writer after taking a one-day course
> in "business writing." I couldn't believe how simple it was. I'll tell you the
> main tricks here so you don't have to waste a day in class."

The sentence-order rule, quoted exactly because the spec inverts it:

> "Learn how brains organize ideas. Readers comprehend "the boy hit the ball"
> quicker than "the ball was hit by the boy." Both sentences mean the same, but
> it's easier to imagine the object (the boy) before the action (the hitting).
> All brains work that way. (Notice I didn't say, "That is the way all brains
> work"?)"

Adams idiosyncratically calls the grammatical subject "the object". His rule is
**actor first, then action** — not "object early", which reverses him. The
spec's P3 bullet "object early in the sentence" is a misreading and must not be
carried forward.

Two things the spec missed:

- **The persuasion-length tradeoff licenses cutting substance, not just waste.**
  "Every sentence pays rent" only ever justifies removing filler. Adams says
  ship the weaker, shorter case because it wins. "Don't fight it" is the
  instruction an agent needs, because an agent will otherwise write the thorough
  version and merely tighten it.
- **One thought per sentence** is the most mechanical construction rule in the
  whole canon, and the spec has no sentence-construction rule at all.

Attribution note: Adams is a contested public figure. The advice stands on its
own, but a published skill file that cites him by name carries a cost the author
should weigh. The rules can be stated without the name.

## 6. Bruce Lee, and the compression maxims

The spec's epigraph is real and correctly quoted, but the famous line is the
weaker half of the idea.

### Lee

Tao of Jeet Kune Do (Ohara, 1975), p. 42:

> "It's not daily increase but daily decrease-hack away the unessentials!"

The fuller passage, from "My View on Gung Fu" in *The Tao of Gung Fu*, which
contains the method rather than the slogan:

> "True gung fu values the wonder of the ordinary, and the cultivation of gung fu
> is not daily increase, but daily decrease. Being wise in gung fu does not mean
> adding more, but to be able to get off with ornamentation and be simply simple
> — like a sculptor building a statue, not by adding but by hacking away the
> unessential so that the truth will be revealed unobstructed."

The sculptor is the useful image: the finished thing is already inside the
draft, and the job is removal, not construction.

Lee's actual criterion for "unessential", from the same essay:

> "To me, the extraordinary aspect of gung fu lies in its simplicity. Gung fu is
> simply the direct expression of one's feeling with the minimum of movements and
> energy. Every movement is being so of itself without the artificialities with
> which people tend to complicate it. The easy way is always the right way, and
> gung fu is nothing at all special; the closer to the true way of gung fu, the
> less wastage of expression there is."

The "closer to the source" wording the spec quotes appears in *Striking
Thoughts*:

> "Simplicity is hacking away the unessential. - It is not daily increase but
> daily decrease - hack away the unessential! The closer to the source, the less
> wastage there is."

The target is ornament, not length. Cut what costs the reader energy without
carrying meaning.

Lee is adapting Laozi, Daodejing ch. 48 — 為學日益，為道日損 — in James Legge's
translation:

> "He who devotes himself to learning (seeks) from day to day to increase (his
> knowledge); he who devotes himself to the Dao (seeks) from day to day to
> diminish (his doing)."

Cite the chain honestly if citing at all.

### The other compression maxims, with correct attributions

Strunk, *The Elements of Style* (1918), Rule 13 — note the second half, which
is almost always dropped and which is the part that matters here:

> "Omit needless words. Vigorous writing is concise. A sentence should contain
> no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, for the same reason
> that a drawing should have no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary
> parts. This requires not that the writer make all his sentences short, or that
> he avoid all detail and treat his subjects only in outline, but that he make
> every word tell."

Strunk explicitly refuses the short-is-better reading. "Make every word tell" is
the rule; brevity is a consequence, not the target.

Arthur Quiller-Couch, "On Style", Lecture XII, 28 January 1914 — the real source
of "murder your darlings", routinely misattributed to Faulkner or King:

> "Whenever you feel an impulse to perpetrate a piece of exceptionally fine
> writing, obey it — whole-heartedly — and delete it before sending your
> manuscript to press. Murder your darlings."

Note what he actually prescribes: write the fine passage, then delete it. The
target is your best-loved material, not obvious filler.

Pascal, *Les Provinciales*, Lettre 16 (4 December 1656) — the correct source of
the "shorter letter" line, usually misattributed to Twain:

> "Je n'ai fait celle-ci plus longue que parce que je n'ai pas eu le loisir de la
> faire plus courte."

E. B. White on Strunk, from his introduction to the 4th edition, on the cost of
the discipline:

> ""Omit needless words!" cries the author on page 23, and into that imperative
> Will Strunk really put his heart and soul."

## 7. Where these sources contradict each other

Recorded because a skill that flattens these seams will be wrong for one genre.

**Openings.** Graham: an essay "doesn't begin with a statement, but with a
question." McEnerney: open by constructing the reader's problem. Adams: the
first sentence must make the reader curious. All three are tension-first. BLUF,
Minto, and Smart Brevity (see the to-ceo notes) are answer-first. The genre
decides, and the skill must say which is which rather than pick a default.

**Passive voice.** Adams argues actor-first via "the boy hit the ball".
McEnerney names the passive-voice rule as the one he would most like to destroy.
Graham never mentions it. The spec presents active voice as a consensus of three
sources; it is not.

**Length.** Strunk explicitly denies that concision means short sentences or
outline-level treatment. Graham says qualification is a skill, not a tax. Caro's
method produces books. None of them supports a word-count target, and the spec's
"final draft must be meaningfully shorter" has no source behind it.

**Simplicity.** Graham: "There are of course times when you want to use a
complicated sentence or fancy word for effect. But you should never do it by
accident." Wallace: a term of art earns its place when nothing else captures the
thing. Both permit complexity chosen deliberately; neither permits it by
default.

## 8. Corrections — errors caught in research, recorded so they stay caught

- **A spliced Caro quote.** An earlier research pass produced a New Yorker Radio
  Hour transcript excerpt in which Caro appears to answer "Yes" to the claim that
  he writes toward a known last line. In the real transcript he chuckles, changes
  the subject for roughly 1,300 words, and gives that "Yes" to a different
  question later. The quote is not used in this file. The Kirkus and C-SPAN2
  material carries the same claim honestly.
- **"Structure backward from the ending"** misdescribes Caro. He fixes the
  destination first and then constructs forward to it.
- **"Object early in the sentence"** inverts Adams, who uses "object" to mean the
  grammatical subject. His rule is actor first, then action.
- **The four factors as a product** is a heuristic drawn from Graham's simile,
  not his stated formula; he writes it with plus signs in his own summary.
- **Machine-generated transcripts** (the C-SPAN2 captions) are marked as such.
  Do not quote them as verbatim text.
